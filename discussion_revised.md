**Population Structure Reflects the Evolutionary History of Cultivated Rice**

A principal finding of this study is that the Rice Diversity Panel exhibits a pronounced hierarchical population structure consistent with that previously documented in large-scale genomic surveys of Asian rice (Wang et al., 2018). Bayesian clustering identified two biologically meaningful levels: a primary subdivision at K = 2, representing the deepest axis of genome-wide evolutionary differentiation, and a secondary subdivision at K = 5, which resolved additional structure within these broader lineages. Rather than representing competing models, these two clustering levels describe successive layers of the same evolutionary history, with the finer subdivision emerging after the deepest historical divergence has been resolved.

The primary ΔK peak at K = 2 is consistent with the hierarchical behavior of the ΔK method (Evanno et al., 2005), which typically identifies the deepest level of population subdivision. For cultivated Asian rice, this primary split consistently corresponds to the long-recognized *indica*/*japonica* divergence recovered across SSR-, SNP array-, and whole-genome sequencing studies (Garris et al., 2005; Zhao et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2018). In the present study, ADMIXTURE at K = 2 partitioned the 379 accessions into two groups of 146 and 233 individuals, corresponding to the *indica*/*aus*/*aromatic* lineage and the *japonica* lineage, respectively. Passport annotation confirmed that ADMIXTURE Cluster 1 contained 77 IND, 49 AUS, and 7 AROMATIC accessions (plus 13 ADMIX), while Cluster 2 contained 93 TEJ and 93 TRJ accessions (plus 41 ADMIX and 6 AROMATIC). This bipartition has recently been confirmed at a much larger scale in the Africa Rice gene bank collection (5,738 accessions, 25,938 SNPs), where model-based, distance-based, and PCA approaches independently recovered the same *indica*/*japonica*/*admixed* partition (Ndjiondjop et al., 2023).

At K = 5, the Bayesian model further resolved five genetically distinct subpopulations that correspond precisely to the widely accepted five-group classification of cultivated Asian rice: *indica* (IND, n = 87), *aus* (AUS, n = 51), temperate *japonica* (TEJ, n = 111), tropical *japonica* (TRJ, n = 115), and *aromatic* (AROMATIC, n = 15). Passport-based subpopulation labels, which were not incorporated into the clustering algorithm, independently validated the ADMIXTURE assignments with high concordance: 88.5% of Cluster 1 were IND, 96.1% of Cluster 3 were AUS, 83.8% of Cluster 4 were TEJ, 80.9% of Cluster 5 were TRJ, and 86.7% of Cluster 2 were AROMATIC. Column purity reached 100% for each of the five major varietal groups, indicating that every passport-classified IND, AUS, TEJ, TRJ, and AROMATIC accession was assigned to a single ADMIXTURE cluster without ambiguity (Table 6). ADMIX (admixed) accessions (n = 54) were distributed across clusters 1, 4, and 5, consistent with their expected intermediate ancestry.

The hierarchical organization from K = 2 to K = 5 parallels rice domestication history. At K = 2, the primary split separates the *indica* lineage (IND + AUS + AROMATIC) from the *japonica* lineage (TEJ + TRJ), reflecting the initial divergence thousands of years ago (Molina et al., 2011). At K = 5, further subdivision resolves AUS as a distinct group within the *indica* lineage — consistent with its independent domestication history in Bangladesh and eastern India — and separates TEJ from TRJ within the *japonica* lineage, reflecting the geographic expansion of *japonica* ancestries into temperate East Asia (TEJ) and tropical Southeast Asia and the Americas (TRJ), together with local adaptation, artificial selection, and demographic bottlenecks (Huang et al., 2012; Alam et al., 2021).

Confidence in the inferred population structure is supported by the convergence of four independent analytical approaches. PCA independently recovered the same hierarchical subdivision: PC1 (24.9% of total variance) clearly separated the *indica* and *japonica* lineages corresponding to K = 2, while PC2 (11.9%) and higher PCs captured additional variation corresponding to the five-group structure at K = 5. Accessions occupying intermediate PCA positions closely matched ADMIX individuals, indicating that both approaches recovered the same evolutionary transitions despite relying on different statistical frameworks. ADMIXTURE cross-validation (K = 5; CV = 0.621) and DAPC (BIC optimum at five clusters; 82.8% of between-group variance on the first two axes) provided additional model-based and multivariate support for the five-group model. DAPC cluster assignments showed 89–100% concordance with ADMIXTURE after label alignment (Table 6), and posterior membership probabilities exceeded 0.9 for 95% of accessions, confirming well-differentiated genetic groups. Similar concordance between multiple analytical approaches has been reported consistently in genome-wide rice diversity studies (Zhao et al., 2011; McCouch et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018).

Neighbor-joining phylogenetic reconstruction provided an additional independent assessment. Although the neighbor-joining method reconstructs evolutionary relationships directly from pairwise genetic distances rather than allele-frequency models (Saitou and Nei, 1987), it nevertheless recovered the same five principal genetic groups, with long internal branches separating major lineages and comparatively short terminal branches within them. This topology is consistent with deep historical divergence among the principal domesticated rice lineages followed by more recent diversification associated with domestication, geographic expansion, local adaptation, and breeding. The convergence of STRUCTURE, ADMIXTURE, DAPC, PCA, and neighbor-joining analyses provides compelling evidence that the inferred clusters represent genuine evolutionary lineages rather than analytical artefacts (Pritchard et al., 2000; Price et al., 2006).
